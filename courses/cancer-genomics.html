Cancer Genomics

Reading a tumour genome, and how much of it anyone understands.

Built from Four CC BY 4.0 reviews: Boysen et al. 2025, Unger and Kather 2024, Huang et al. 2023, and Iniguez-Munoz et al. 2024.

Read Boysen et al. 2025 on mutational signaturesRead Unger and Kather 2024 on deep learning in cancer genomicsRead Huang et al. 2023 on single cell RNA sequencingRead Iniguez-Munoz et al. 2024 on non-coding mutations

What this course is for

Sequencing a tumour is now routine. Understanding what comes back is not. This course is about the second part: what a cancer genome actually contains, how the patterns in it are read, and how much of what is read is genuinely understood.

It is built on four review papers, all free to read in full. Boysen and colleagues, writing in Nucleic Acids Research in 2025, cover mutational signatures and where they come from. Unger and Kather, in Genome Medicine in 2024, cover what deep learning can and cannot do with genomic and histopathology data. Huang and colleagues, in the Journal of Hematology and Oncology in 2023, cover single cell sequencing. Iniguez-Munoz and colleagues, in Cellular and Molecular Life Sciences in 2024, cover the mutations that fall outside genes entirely.

One of the authors of the first paper is Ludmil Alexandrov, who originated the mutational signature framework the whole field now uses. That is about as close to the source as a review gets.

Think of it like this

Course one asked what a mutation is. This one asks what a hundred thousand of them, read at once, can tell you.

A tumour genome is not a list of faults. It is a record of everything that has happened to a cell, and most of it is readable.

The words you need first

Fifteen terms. The rest of the course is written using these and very little beyond them.

Glossary

Kinds of change in a genome

Seven kinds of thing that can be different about a tumour genome. The catalogues in this course are organised by them.

Single base substitution
Shortened to SBS. One letter replaced by another. The commonest kind of change in cancer.
Doublet base substitution
Shortened to DBS. Two neighbouring letters changed at the same time.
Insertion or deletion
Shortened to indel. A short stretch of DNA added or lost, usually fewer than fifty letters.
Copy number change
A whole region gained or lost, so a gene is present in more or fewer copies than the two you should have.
Structural variant
A large rearrangement: a deletion, duplication, inversion or translocation, typically over a thousand letters.
Somatic and germline
A somatic change happened in your lifetime and is only in some cells. A germline change was inherited and is in all of them.
Driver and passenger
A driver gives the tumour an advantage. A passenger came along for the ride. Most changes are passengers.
Glossary

Reading and interpreting

Eight more, and then the biology.

Trinucleotide context
The letter before and the letter after the one that changed. The same substitution means different things in different contexts, and this is the whole basis of signature analysis.
Mutational signature
A recurring pattern of changes left behind by one process, whether a chemical, a broken repair system or simple ageing.
The COSMIC catalogue
The Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer. The public reference list of signatures and of the genes implicated in cancer.
Variant calling
Comparing a sequenced sample against a reference to work out what is different. The first step of almost every analysis.
Gene regulatory element
Shortened to GRE. A stretch of DNA that controls when a gene is read, without coding for anything itself.
Whole slide image
Shortened to WSI. A scan of a stained tissue slide, often billions of pixels.
Bulk and single cell
Bulk sequencing averages every cell in a sample together. Single cell sequencing reads them one at a time.
Supervised and unsupervised
Supervised learning is trained on labelled examples. Unsupervised learning is given the data and asked to find structure in it.

Why a mutation happens at all

Start with the machinery, because everything later is a departure from it.

The copying is extraordinarily good

A human cell copies three billion letters of DNA every time it divides, and gets almost all of them right. Boysen and colleagues put the error rate as low as one mistake in a billion bases replicated, which is a level of accuracy no manufactured process reaches.

That accuracy is not one mechanism. It is three, working in sequence. The polymerase choosing the right base gets it wrong about once in ten thousand to a hundred thousand. Its own proofreading, an exonuclease activity that reads backwards along the strand it just made, catches most of what remains. Mismatch repair then goes over the finished copy afterwards and catches most of what got past that.

Understanding this is the point of the section, because every mutational process in this course is a way of defeating one of those three layers. A chemical that sticks to DNA defeats the first. A broken proofreading domain defeats the second. A mismatch repair deficiency defeats the third, and each of them leaves a different pattern behind.

Think of it like this

Three separate proofreads of the same manuscript, by three people with different habits. Knowing which one was skipped tells you what kind of typos to expect.

Nothing here is fragile. Cancer is what happens when a system this good is run for long enough.

And a way past all three

There is a fourth situation the three layers do not cover. When a chemical has stuck to a base, forming what is called an adduct, the normal polymerase physically cannot get past it. The replication fork stalls, and a stalled fork kills the cell.

So cells carry specialist polymerases for exactly this, in a process called translesion synthesis. They can copy past a damaged base. What they cannot do is check their work: they generally lack the proofreading exonuclease. Boysen and colleagues give their error rates as anywhere from a thousandth of a per cent to a hundred per cent, depending on which adduct is being bypassed and what surrounds it.

This is the mechanism that makes signatures readable. A particular chemical makes a particular adduct, a particular polymerase bypasses it in a particular way, and the mistake it makes depends on the neighbouring letters. The pattern in the genome is a fingerprint of that whole chain.

Surviving the damage is what creates the mutation. A cell that could not bypass the lesion would simply die instead.

Mutational signatures

The single most useful idea in cancer genomics, and the one most worth understanding properly.

Six substitutions, ninety six contexts

There are only six possible single base substitutions, and by convention each is named after the pyrimidine of the pair: C to A, C to G, C to T, T to A, T to C and T to G. Six categories would tell you almost nothing.

The trick is context. Take the letter immediately before and the letter immediately after the one that changed, and each of the six becomes sixteen, giving ninety six categories rather than six. Now the patterns separate. A cytosine changing to thymine means one thing when it sits between a thymine and a guanine, and quite another between an adenine and a thymine.

Boysen and colleagues put the underlying reason plainly: the identity of the flanking nucleotides influences how likely that mutation is in the first place. Context is not a labelling convenience. It is part of the chemistry.

Think of it like this

Six letters of an alphabet tell you nothing. The same six letters with what comes before and after each one is a language.

Ninety six numbers per tumour. That is the entire input to signature analysis.

How the signatures were found, and what the maths is doing

Every tumour genome is a mixture. Several mutational processes have been running in it, at different times and different rates, and what you observe is all of them added together. The problem is to work backwards from the sum to the parts, without knowing in advance what the parts are.

The method used is non-negative matrix factorisation, an unsupervised machine learning technique. Given the ninety six category profiles of a few thousand tumours, it finds a small set of underlying patterns which, combined in different proportions, reconstruct all of them. Non-negative matters: a process can contribute mutations or contribute none, but it cannot contribute a negative number, and building that constraint into the maths is what makes the answers interpretable.

Boysen and colleagues give the standard for a good reconstruction: the cosine similarity between the rebuilt profile and the original should be at least 0.90. The framework was then applied at scale by the International Cancer Genome Consortium, The Cancer Genome Atlas and the Pan-Cancer Analysis of Whole Genomes network, using data from over twenty three thousand cancer patients.

Think of it like this

A recording of an orchestra, and the task is to work out which instruments were playing and how loudly, given only the sound they made together.

Nobody told the algorithm what tobacco looks like. It found the pattern, and the epidemiology was matched to it afterwards.
