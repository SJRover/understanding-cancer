Cancer Immunology and Immunotherapy

Why the immune system misses cancer, and what the drugs actually do.

Built from Three CC BY 4.0 reviews: Tufail et al. 2025, Marei et al. 2023, and Jenkins et al. 2023.

Read Tufail et al. 2025 on immune evasionRead Marei et al. 2023 on checkpoint inhibitors and resistanceRead Jenkins et al. 2023 on T cell exhaustion

What this course is for

Your immune system kills abnormal cells constantly and you never notice. A cancer is, among other things, a group of cells that has found a way to stop that happening. This course is about how they do it, and about the drugs built to undo it.

It is built on three review papers, all free to read in full. Tufail, Jiang and Li, writing in Signal Transduction and Targeted Therapy in 2025, cover the mechanisms of immune evasion. Marei, Hasan, Pozzoli and Cenciarelli, in Cancer Cell International in 2023, cover what checkpoint inhibitors achieve and why they stop working. Jenkins, Whitehead, Fellermeyer, Davis and Sharma, in Oxford Open Immunology in 2023, cover T cell exhaustion. All three are linked from this course.

Where a finding rests on a single experiment in a single model, the slide will say so. That distinction matters more than it sounds: a review mentioning one mouse study in passing is not making the same kind of claim as one reporting a trial result, and learning to hear the difference is part of what this course is for.

Think of it like this

The first four courses were about what a cancer cell does. This one is about what everything around it fails to do.

A tumour is not invisible to the immune system. It is a tumour that has already survived being seen.

What you should already know

This course assumes the first two courses in the series. You need to know what a mutation is, what a clone is, and why a cell dividing when it should not is a problem, which is What Is Cancer. You need the hallmarks framework, and in particular the hallmark called evading immune destruction, which is The Hallmarks of Cancer.

You do not need any prior immunology. Every immune cell and molecule used here is defined before it is used, and the vocabulary slide comes before the biology rather than after it.

The words you need first

Fourteen terms. The rest of the course is written using these and very little beyond them.

Glossary

The immune cells

Eight cell types. Four of them are on your side and four have been turned.

T cell
A white blood cell that kills infected or abnormal cells, or helps other immune cells do it. The central character in this whole course.
CD8 positive T cell
The killer. Also called a cytotoxic T lymphocyte, shortened to CTL. It recognises a target and destroys it.
CD4 positive T cell
The helper. It does not usually kill. It instructs and sustains the cells that do.
Natural killer cell
Shortened to NK cell. Kills abnormal cells without needing to be shown a specific target first.
Dendritic cell
The cell that picks up fragments of a tumour and shows them to T cells. If this step fails, nothing downstream happens.
Regulatory T cell
Shortened to Treg. A CD4 positive T cell carrying the FoxP3 protein whose job is to hold the immune system back. Useful in health, exploited in cancer.
Myeloid derived suppressor cell
Shortened to MDSC. An immature myeloid cell that expands in cancer and shuts T cells down.
Tumour associated macrophage
Shortened to TAM. A macrophage recruited into a tumour and repurposed to help it.
Glossary

The molecules and the places

Six more, and then the biology.

Antigen
Anything the immune system can recognise. A tumour antigen is one found on tumour cells.
Neoantigen
A brand new antigen, created by a mutation, that has never existed in the body before. These are the ones the immune system can genuinely call foreign.
Major histocompatibility complex
Shortened to MHC. The surface display case. MHC class I shows what is inside a cell to CD8 positive T cells. If a cell stops displaying, it stops being checked.
Immune checkpoint
A molecule that turns an immune response up or down. The word covers both brakes and accelerators, which is the single commonest source of confusion here.
Tumour microenvironment
Shortened to TME. Everything in the tumour that is not a cancer cell: blood vessels, connective tissue, immune cells and the chemical soup they sit in.
T cell exhaustion
The state a T cell enters after being stimulated for too long. It is still there. It has stopped working properly.

The immune system already shapes every tumour you will ever see

Before any of the evasion mechanisms, the finding that reframes them.

Elimination, equilibrium, escape

Tufail, Jiang and Li describe immune editing as a process in three phases. In the elimination phase the innate and adaptive immune systems together destroy early tumour cells, with dendritic cells activating T cells to do the killing. Some cells survive that and enter the equilibrium phase, where they sit dormant or divide slowly under continuous immune surveillance. In the escape phase, the cells that have acquired changes letting them avoid recognition take over and grow unopposed.

The consequence is uncomfortable and worth sitting with. Every tumour a clinician ever sees is, by definition, one that got through all three phases. The immune system is not absent from the story of that tumour. It is one of the forces that selected it.

This is why the phrase evading immune destruction is a hallmark rather than an accident. A tumour that had not solved this problem would not be a tumour you could find.

Think of it like this

Every burglar you catch is bad at burglary. The ones who are good at it are the reason you never see them.

The tumour in front of you is the survivor of a selection you did not observe.

The two experiments that made it more than a story

The review cites two pieces of evidence, and both are worth knowing because they are the kind of experiment that turns an appealing idea into a testable one.

The first: tumours induced by the chemical MCA in mice lacking Rag2, which cannot make working T cells or B cells, were more immunogenic when transplanted into normal mice than tumours raised in normal mice were. In other words, a tumour that grew without immune pressure never learned to hide, and it showed. Tumours that grow with an immune system present have already had their most visible versions removed.

The second is more specific. In a sarcoma cell line called d42m1, pressure from T cells produced escape variants that had lost the strong rejection antigen. The original tumour carried a point mutation in the gene for Spectrin beta 2 that produced a neoantigen. The escape variants did not have it. The immune system had not just been evaded, it had done the editing.

The immune system does not only fail to stop cancers. It changes which cancers exist.

Four ways out

The organising structure of the primary source, and the map for the rest of this course.

Diagram

The four families of escape

One: make the immune cells stop working

The most direct route is chemical. Tumour cells release transforming growth factor beta, shortened to TGF beta, which restricts the activation and growth of both T cells and natural killer cells, and pushes naive CD4 positive T cells to become regulatory T cells instead. They release interleukin 10, shortened to IL-10, which blocks pro inflammatory signals from macrophages and dendritic cells and so prevents T cells being activated in the first place. They release vascular endothelial growth factor, shortened to VEGF, which is mostly known for growing blood vessels but which also stops dendritic cells maturing.

Then they recruit cells whose job is suppression. Regulatory T cells accumulate and release IL-10 and TGF beta and carry CTLA-4. Myeloid derived suppressor cells expand and shut T cells down using reactive oxygen species, nitric oxide and arginase, and by stripping the local area of the nutrients T cells need.

The part that surprises people is that waste products do this too. The review reports that lactic acid reduces the killing activity of cytotoxic T lymphocytes by up to fifty per cent, and that the effect reverses after twenty four hours in medium without it. That last detail matters more than the first: it means the T cells were not destroyed, they were held down, and removing the pressure let them recover.

Think of it like this

Not a fight the tumour wins. An atmosphere in which the other side cannot operate.

Lactic acid, the by product of a tumour growing quickly, is itself one of the tumour's defences.

Two: pull the brakes

Immune checkpoints exist so that an immune response can be stopped. Without them, every infection would end in autoimmune disease. Tumours use them exactly as designed, which is what makes this route so hard to counter without side effects.

The two best known are programmed cell death protein 1, shortened to PD-1, with its partner PD-L1, and cytotoxic T lymphocyte associated antigen 4, shortened to CTLA-4. But the review lists many more that are now being targeted: lymphocyte activation gene 3, shortened to LAG-3, which binds MHC class II; T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain containing 3, shortened to TIM-3, which binds galectin 9; and T cell immunoreceptor with immunoglobulin and ITIM domains, shortened to TIGIT, which competes with the activating receptor CD226 for the same ligands.

One of them is not about T cells at all. CD47 is the do not eat me signal. It binds SIRP alpha on macrophages and tells them this cell is self. Tumours raise CD47 to avoid being eaten, which is a different immune system entirely from the one PD-1 drugs address.

Tumours did not invent any of these. They are standard equipment, used as intended.

Brakes and accelerators are both called checkpoints

Three: change the neighbourhood

The tumour microenvironment is not a passive setting. Tumours grow faster than their blood supply, which leaves regions short of oxygen. That hypoxia stabilises hypoxia inducible factor 1 alpha, shortened to HIF-1 alpha, which then does several unhelpful things at once: it binds the PD-L1 promoter and raises PD-L1, it turns MHC class I down, it recruits myeloid derived suppressor cells, and it pushes macrophages towards the suppressive M2 state.

There is also a physical component that is easy to underrate. Cancer associated fibroblasts remodel the extracellular matrix using enzymes called matrix metalloproteinases. The collagen network that results is a barrier: the review is explicit that it limits T cell mobility and lowers their killing power against tumour cells. A T cell that cannot physically reach a tumour cell is as useless as one that cannot recognise it.

The evidence that this is causal rather than incidental is a pancreatic experiment the review describes. Inhibiting the enzyme GFAT1 lowered hyaluronan and collagen in the matrix, which raised CD8 positive T cell infiltration, which in turn made tumours that had not responded to anti PD-1 therapy respond to it.

Think of it like this

Two different problems that look the same from outside. One is a locked door. The other is a corridor too narrow to walk down.

Some tumours are cold because nothing recognises them. Others are cold because nothing can get in.
