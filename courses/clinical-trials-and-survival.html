Clinical Trials and Survival

How cancer evidence is made, and how to read it without being misled.

Built from Five CC BY 4.0 papers: Msaouel et al. 2023, Cuzick and Sasieni 2022, Ko et al. 2021, Parikh et al. 2023, and Sjolander and Dickman 2024.

What this course is for

Every previous course in this series ends at the same place: a result from a trial. This one is about where those results come from, what the numbers in them mean, and the specific ways a trial can produce a true number that supports a false conclusion.

It is built on five papers, all free to read in full. Msaouel, Lee and Thall, writing in Cancers in 2023, is the backbone and covers how to interpret a randomised trial. Cuzick and Sasieni, in the British Journal of Cancer in 2022, cover noninferiority. Ko and colleagues, in Biomarker Research in 2021, cover how tumour response is measured. Parikh and colleagues, in JAMA Oncology in 2023, report what happened when drugs were approved on endpoints that later failed to confirm. Sjolander and Dickman, in the American Journal of Epidemiology in 2024, are on model assumptions.

Two of those author lists are worth noticing. Peter Thall is one of the field's senior biostatisticians, and Jack Cuzick spent a career on cancer trial methodology. These are not summaries at a distance.

Think of it like this

The other courses teach you what is known. This one teaches you how anybody came to know it.

A trial result is not a fact. It is a measurement, made under conditions, and the conditions are half the meaning.

What you should already know

No statistics background is assumed. Confidence intervals, hazard ratios and p-values are all defined before they are used, and the emphasis throughout is on reading them rather than computing them.

It helps to have done at least one of the earlier courses, because the examples are drawn from cancer and assume you know roughly what a tumour is doing. Course six mentions response rates and course seven mentions survival predictions, and both point forwards to here.

There is no mathematics in this course. Not because it does not matter, but because the useful skill for most readers is being able to tell a well conducted trial from a badly interpreted one, and that skill is mostly not mathematical.

The words you need first

Fourteen terms. Everything after this is written using them.

Glossary

How a trial is built

Seven words about how a trial is put together.

Randomised controlled trial
Shortened to RCT. Patients are allocated to treatments by chance rather than by choice, which is what makes the groups comparable.
Control arm
The group that gets the existing standard of care, or a placebo. What the new thing is being measured against.
Blinding
Keeping patients, clinicians or assessors unaware of which treatment was given, so that knowing cannot change what is reported.
Intention to treat
Shortened to ITT. Analysing patients by the group they were assigned to, whether or not they actually took the treatment.
Per protocol
Shortened to PP. Analysing patients by what they actually received.
Crossover
Allowing patients in the control arm to switch to the new treatment, usually after their disease progresses.
Censoring
A patient whose outcome has not happened yet when the trial stops counting. They are not a failure and not a success, and how they are handled matters a great deal.
Glossary

What comes out of it

Seven more, about what a trial reports.

Hazard ratio
Shortened to HR. Roughly, the rate at which the event happens in one group divided by the rate in the other. Below 1 favours the new treatment. Above 1 favours the control.
Confidence interval
Shortened to CI. The range of values compatible with the data. Its width is usually more informative than the estimate in the middle of it.
p-value
How surprising the data would be if there were genuinely no difference. It is not the probability that the treatment works, and reading it that way is the single most common statistical error in medicine.
Endpoint
The thing being measured. Which one was chosen often decides the answer.
Surrogate endpoint
Something measured early, standing in for an outcome that would take years to observe.
Overall survival
Shortened to OS. Time until death from any cause.
Progression free survival
Shortened to PFS. Time until the disease worsens or the patient dies, whichever comes first.

What randomisation actually buys you

Less than people assume, and something more specific.

It does not make the groups the same

The usual explanation is that randomisation makes the two groups comparable. That is loosely true and precisely wrong. Any particular randomisation will leave the groups differing in hundreds of ways, some of them relevant.

What random allocation actually does is remove every systematic influence on which treatment a patient was assigned. Nobody chose. No doctor put the fitter patients on the new drug, and no patient selected themselves into the arm they preferred. That is a narrower claim and a far stronger one, because it is what makes the uncertainty estimates mean anything.

Msaouel, Lee and Thall make the consequence explicit and it is worth carrying. Random allocation physically justifies the statistical comparison. Without it, a difference between two groups might be the treatment, or might be whatever made one group different in the first place, and no amount of statistical adjustment reliably separates the two.

Think of it like this

Not a guarantee that the two teams are equally good. A guarantee that nobody picked the sides.

Randomisation does not balance the groups. It removes the reason to think anyone tilted them.

Assigned, received, and used are three different things

Patients do not always do what a trial assigns. Analysing by assignment is intention to treat. Analysing by what patients actually received is per protocol. These answer genuinely different questions and neither is simply better.

The clearest example in the source is a colonoscopy screening trial. Patients were randomly allocated either to receive an invitation to a single screening colonoscopy or to receive nothing. The intention to treat analysis found the risk of colorectal cancer at ten years reduced in the invited group, with a risk ratio of 0.82 and a 95 per cent confidence interval from 0.70 to 0.93.

But only 42 per cent of invited patients actually had the colonoscopy. The per protocol analysis, adjusted, gave a risk ratio of 0.69 with a confidence interval from 0.55 to 0.83. Both numbers are correct. The first answers what happens if a health service sends out invitations. The second is closer to what happens if you personally have the procedure. Which one you want depends entirely on whether you are running a screening programme or sitting in a clinic.

Intention to treat answers a policy question. Per protocol answers a clinical one. Confusing them is not a small error.

Why overall survival is the one everybody trusts

Msaouel, Lee and Thall are unambiguous about it. Of all the clinical endpoints used in medical trials, overall survival is the least ambiguous and the least subject to measurement bias. Death is not a matter of interpretation. Nobody needs to measure it on a scan, and no assessor can be influenced about it.

Everything else requires a judgement. Whether a tumour has progressed depends on a measurement of an image, made by a person, against a set of rules. That is why the rules matter so much, and why the next section is about them.

But overall survival has a serious practical problem, which is that it takes years, and a second problem that is less well known and more damaging.

Death is the only endpoint that nobody has to interpret.

How a later treatment can wreck an earlier comparison

Suppose a trial randomises patients between two first line treatments. Some progress, and are then given something else. Their total survival time is now the time to progression, which depends on the randomised treatment, plus the time from progression to death, which depends on whatever they were given afterwards.

That second treatment was not randomised. It was chosen by a clinician, based on how the patient was doing, which means the usual confounding of an observational study has been reintroduced into the back half of a randomised trial. Msaouel, Lee and Thall put it directly: it would be misleading to analyse overall survival from such trials using approaches meant for trials with completely randomised allocation.

This is why intermediate endpoints such as progression free survival are, in one specific sense, on firmer ground than overall survival. The comparison up to the point of progression is protected by the original randomisation. What happens after it is not.

Think of it like this

A fair race, followed by an unrecorded second leg in which the runners chose their own routes.

Overall survival is the most reliable endpoint to measure and sometimes the hardest to attribute.
