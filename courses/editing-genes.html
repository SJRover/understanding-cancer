Editing Genes

Reading, silencing and rewriting the genome, and what has reached patients.

Built from Four CC BY 4.0 reviews: Xu et al. 2024, Samuels et al. 2025, Adly et al. 2026, and Guo 2026.

Where this course sits

Four courses have got you here. The first said cancer is a consequence of somatic mutation and clonal selection. The second said a cancer is defined by a set of capabilities it has to acquire. The third said the same machinery underlies aging. The fourth said how any of that is known.

This one asks the obvious next question. If cancer is caused by specific changes in specific genes, and we now have tools that can change specific genes, what actually happens when you point one at the other?

It is built from four papers, all free to read in full: a 2024 review of base editing and prime editing by Xu and colleagues in the Journal of Translational Medicine, a 2025 review of CRISPR screening in breast cancer by Samuels and colleagues in Cancer and Metastasis Reviews, a 2026 review of chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapy by Adly and colleagues in MedComm, and a 2026 review of CRISPR interference by Guo in the International Journal of Molecular Sciences.

Think of it like this

The first four courses were about reading the machine. This one is about the first tools that can reach inside it while it is still running.

Four courses to establish that cancer is genetic. This one asks what follows from that.

A warning about the literature, before anything else

Gene editing has the most enthusiastic press of any field in biology, and it is worth arriving braced for it. Three of the four papers behind this course are reviews written by people who work on the technology. That does not make them wrong, and all four are notably careful about their own limits, but it does shape what gets emphasised.

The discipline this course applies is simple. Where a source states a limitation, the slide states it in the same strength of language the source used. Where a source describes something as predicted, potential or expected rather than demonstrated, the slide keeps that word. Guo, for instance, writes that based on these studies we can predict certain results from silencing particular genes, which is a hypothesis and is presented here as one.

The other habit worth having is to ask, of any editing result, three questions. Was it in cells, in mice or in people? Was the edit made outside the body and the cells put back, or made inside the body? And how many patients? The answers change what the result means far more than the elegance of the technique does.

Think of it like this

Read a gene editing paper the way you would read a company announcing its own results. Not dishonestly, but knowing which direction the emphasis will lean.

In cells, in mice, or in people. Outside the body or inside. How many. Three questions, asked every time.

Four tools, and the difference that matters

Cutting, converting, rewriting and silencing. The distinction is whether the DNA gets broken.

Why cutting DNA is the crude option

The original CRISPR-Cas9 system works by cutting. A guide RNA (ribonucleic acid) directs the Cas9 enzyme to a matching sequence and it makes a double strand break, cutting straight through the DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid). And then, crucially, Cas9 stops. It cannot repair anything. What happens next is entirely up to the cell.

The cell has two ways to fix a double strand break. Non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) simply glues the two ends back together, and Xu and colleagues describe it plainly as error-prone: it often leaves small insertions or deletions, and in a coding sequence those shift the reading frame and destroy the gene. That is useful if destroying the gene is what you wanted. Homology-directed repair (HDR) is the precise route, copying from a template, but it is predominantly active in dividing cells, which makes it ineffective in most therapeutically relevant cell types.

So the honest summary is that Cas9 is a good tool for breaking a gene and a poor tool for fixing one. Worse, the review notes that off-target cuts can produce deletions, inversions or translocations, triggering DNA damage responses and increasing the risk of oncogenesis. Using a cutting tool to treat cancer carries a small risk of causing it.

Think of it like this

It is a hole punch, not a pen. You can reliably remove something. Putting a specific thing in its place is somebody else's job, and that somebody is careless.

Cas9 cuts and then leaves. Everything after the cut is the cell improvising.

Base editing: chemistry instead of cutting

A base editor sidesteps the whole problem. It is a fusion protein: a Cas9 that has been crippled so it can only nick one strand rather than cut both, joined to an enzyme that chemically converts one DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) letter into another.

Two kinds came first. Cytosine base editors (CBEs) convert C to T, using a cytidine deaminase such as APOBEC1 together with a uracil glycosylase inhibitor (UGI) domain to stop the cell undoing the work. Adenine base editors (ABEs) convert A to G, using an adenine deaminase evolved in the laboratory from a transfer RNA (ribonucleic acid) enzyme to work on single stranded DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid), which is not what it originally did.

The gain is real: higher efficiency than Cas9, far fewer insertion and deletion byproducts, and none of the consequences that follow a double strand break. The cost is flexibility. Xu and colleagues are explicit that base editors have not achieved all 12 possible point mutation types, and cannot perform insertions, deletions or most conversions. A base editor is a very precise instrument for a narrow set of jobs.

Think of it like this

It is a chemical correction rather than a cut and splice. The page is never torn, so nobody has to tape it back together.

No break, no donor template, far fewer accidents. In exchange, only certain letters can be changed into certain others.

Prime editing: writing rather than converting

Prime editing is the more ambitious idea and the more complicated machine. Again a Cas9 nickase, this time fused to a reverse transcriptase, the enzyme that writes DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) from an RNA (ribonucleic acid) template. And the guide is not just an address: a prime editing guide RNA (pegRNA) carries both the target location and the sequence you want written there.

The mechanism is worth following once. The editor nicks the target, which frees a strand end. That end pairs with a primer binding site on the pegRNA. The reverse transcriptase then uses the rest of the pegRNA as a template and writes the new sequence directly into the genome, and the cell's repair machinery incorporates it.

Xu and colleagues make an argument about why this is more specific than it looks: prime editing requires three independent hybridisation events rather than one, and that requirement for multiple base pairing steps greatly enhances flexibility, editing purity and targeting specificity. The honest counterweight, in their own words, is that prime editing efficiency varies widely and is often low, depending on the edit, the target sequence and the cell type.

Think of it like this

Base editing changes a letter that is already on the page. Prime editing brings its own text and writes it in.

Three separate matching steps have to succeed. That is both why it is specific and why it often does not work.

CRISPRi: not editing at all

The fourth tool is the odd one out, and it is the one this course returns to at the end because it changes what the word editing means. CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) uses a dead Cas9 (dCas9), made by mutating both nuclease domains, D10A and H840A, so it can still find and grip DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) but cannot cut it at all.

Parked on a gene's promoter, dCas9 is a physical obstruction: it blocks RNA (ribonucleic acid) polymerase from attaching or from moving along. Guo reports that this steric blocking alone typically gives 60 to 80 per cent knockdown, which is useful but variable. For stronger silencing, dCas9 is fused to a repressor domain, most commonly the Kruppel-associated box (KRAB), which recruits the co-repressor KAP1 and through it the machinery that condenses the local chromatin into an inaccessible state.

So the gene is not changed. Not one letter of the sequence is different. It has simply been switched off, and Guo describes this as potent and reversible silencing without altering the underlying genetic code.

Think of it like this

It is the difference between deleting a paragraph and putting a heavy object on top of the page. The words are still there. Nobody can read them.

A gene silenced without a single letter changed. That distinction carries most of the safety argument for CRISPRi.

Test yourself

Think about it: choosing the right tool

  1. You want to correct a single disease-causing letter in a non-dividing cell, such as a neuron. Which tool, and why not the others?

    A base editor, if the required change is one of the conversions it can make. Cas9 with homology-directed repair is out, because Xu and colleagues state that homology-directed repair is predominantly active in mitotic cells and therefore ineffective in most therapeutically relevant cell types, which includes neurons. Prime editing is the fallback if the required change is one a base editor cannot make, accepting the lower and more variable efficiency. CRISPRi is out because it silences rather than corrects, and a mutated gene silenced is still a mutated gene.

  2. MYC is described as undruggable because it has no pocket for a small molecule to bind. Why can CRISPRi target it when a drug cannot?

    Because the two are working on different objects. A small molecule has to bind the finished protein, and Guo explains that MYC lacks a traditional enzymatic pocket for one to grip. CRISPRi never touches the protein: it targets the MYC promoter or its distal enhancers and stops the protein being made at all. Guo describes this as collapsing the transcriptional network. The shape of the protein stops being the obstacle because the protein stops being the target.

The research use: asking every gene at once

CRISPR screening, which is the most productive use of these tools and the one you are most likely to meet.

The idea, which is better than it sounds

Most of what these tools have contributed to cancer biology has come not from editing a patient but from a laboratory experiment with an unusually elegant design. It is worth understanding properly because the logic generalises.

You build a library of guide RNAs (ribonucleic acids), one set for every gene in the genome. Samuels and colleagues note that libraries typically contain three to ten guides per gene, with most studies using four to six. You put that library into a population of cancer cells using virus, at a deliberately low dose.

The dose is the clever part. Transduction is done at a low multiplicity of infection, typically around 0.3, so that on average each cell receives only one guide. You have now made a population in which every cell has a different single gene disabled, and every cell carries a sequenceable barcode saying which one.

Think of it like this

It is an experiment where the sample size is the genome. Instead of asking whether one gene matters, you ask all of them simultaneously and let the answer sort itself out.

One cell, one gene knocked out, one barcode. Twenty thousand experiments in the same flask.
