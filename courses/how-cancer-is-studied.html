How Cancer Is Studied

Organoids, liquid biopsy and single cells: how anybody knows any of it.

Built from Four CC BY 4.0 reviews: Zhang and He 2026, the ISLB terminology framework 2026, Guzman and Rodriguez 2026, and Carneiro et al. 2025.

Read Zhang and He 2026 on organoidsRead The ISLB liquid biopsy terminology frameworkRead Guzman and Rodriguez 2026 on long read sequencingRead Carneiro et al. 2025 on microfluidics

Why this course exists

The three courses before this one describe what cancer is, what a cancer has to become, and how all of it ties to aging. None of them says how anybody found out. That is the gap this course fills, and it is not a footnote: in a viva or a seminar, the question that separates a good answer from a competent one is almost always some version of how do you know that.

It is built from four papers, all free to read in full: a 2026 review of organoid technology by Zhang and He in Molecular Biomedicine, the International Society of Liquid Biopsy (ISLB) terminology framework published in 2026 in The Journal of Liquid Biopsy, a 2026 review of long read sequencing for liquid biopsy by Guzman and Rodriguez in Frontiers in Oncology, and a 2025 review of microfluidics for single cell cancer research by Carneiro and colleagues in Advanced Science.

Four papers rather than one changes how you should read this course. Where a claim comes from a particular paper, the slide names it. Where the papers cover the same ground from different angles, which happens often between the organoid review and the microfluidics review, the deck says so rather than blending them into one voice.

Think of it like this

It is the difference between knowing that a bridge holds and knowing how it was tested. The first is a fact you were told. The second is a thing you can reason with.

Every claim in courses one to three came from somewhere. This is the course about the somewhere.

The number that explains the whole field

Start with the failure, because every technique in this course is a response to it. Zhang and He put it bluntly: over 90 per cent of agents that show efficacy in preclinical models fail in human clinical trials. Not fail to be blockbusters. Fail.

The cost of that failure is carried by the drugs that do get through. The same review gives an estimated 2 to 3 billion dollars required to bring a new cancer drug to market, and describes a slowing rate of new approvals despite massive investment.

The diagnosis they offer for this is not that the biology is too hard. It is that the models were wrong. Two dimensional cell lines lose tissue structure, clonal diversity, and drift genetically over long culture. Mouse xenografts keep the structure but cost a great deal, run at low throughput, replace the human stroma with mouse stroma, and require an immunodeficient host, which makes studying the immune system impossible by construction.

Think of it like this

Ninety per cent of a wind tunnel's predictions failing in the air would not be treated as bad luck. It would be treated as evidence that the wind tunnel was not modelling the air.

Over 90 per cent of cancer drugs that work in the lab fail in people. Everything here is an attempt to close that gap.

Growing a patient's tumour in a dish

Organoids: what they are, why they are not just cells in a blob, and the trick that makes them pure.

What an organoid actually is

An organoid is not a clump of cells. Zhang and He are explicit that organoids transcend simple three dimensional spheroids by functioning as miniature, self-organised tissue structures. The operative word is self-organised: nobody builds the structure. A population of initially disorganised stem cells spontaneously forms ordered, tissue specific structures without any external scaffold telling them where to go.

Two mechanisms drive that. The first is differential cell adhesion, first described by Steinberg in the 1960s, which sorts different cell types into the right spatial positions. The second is spatially restricted lineage commitment, in which neighbouring cells signal to each other and create local gradients that direct differentiation, the way Paneth cells at the base of an intestinal crypt secrete Wnt and Notch ligands to keep the adjacent stem cells in a stem state.

The reason this matters for cancer is that the self-organising property survives in tumour cells. Tumour organoids recapitulate the histological architecture, cellular heterogeneity and polarity of the original patient tumour, including glandular structures in colorectal and gastric adenocarcinoma organoids and squamous differentiation in head and neck and oesophageal cancer organoids.

Think of it like this

It is closer to a crystal than to a sculpture. Nobody carves a snowflake. The shape is a consequence of what the pieces do when you let them.

Nobody builds an organoid. You supply the conditions and the cells build it.

The four things you have to get right

Zhang and He set out four foundational pillars, and it is worth knowing them because every organoid protocol you will ever read is a variation on these. The first is the stem cell source, either adult stem cells (ASCs) taken from a tissue sample, or pluripotent stem cells (PSCs), which means embryonic stem cells (ESCs) or induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs).

The second is the self-organisation just described. The third is the signalling: four evolutionarily conserved pathways have to be pushed or held back in the right combination. Wnt and beta-catenin signalling is the master regulator of stem cell self-renewal, enhanced in culture by adding Wnt3a and R-spondin 1. Epidermal growth factor (EGF) drives proliferation. Notch decides cell fate. Transforming growth factor beta (TGF-beta) and bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signalling has to be inhibited, which is why the BMP (bone morphogenetic protein) inhibitor Noggin is in almost every epithelial organoid medium.

The fourth is the extracellular matrix (ECM), the jelly the cells sit in. Most protocols use Matrigel, a basement membrane extract from a mouse sarcoma, rich in laminin, collagen IV, entactin and heparan sulfate proteoglycans. It is also, in the review's own words, a poorly defined, xenogeneic product with significant batch to batch variability, which is a problem this course returns to.

Think of it like this

Sourdough is the same shape of problem. Flour, water, temperature and time, and the interesting structure is something the ingredients do rather than something you did.

Source, self-organisation, signalling, scaffold. Every protocol is those four, tuned.

The trick that makes a tumour organoid pure

Here is the most elegant idea in the organoid review, and it is one of those pieces of laboratory reasoning that is worth stealing for other problems. If you take a tumour sample and culture it, you get tumour cells and normal cells together, and separating them by hand is slow and lossy.

So you do not separate them. You starve the normal ones. Normal epithelial cells need the full set of niche factors to survive: Wnt, R-spondin, EGF (epidermal growth factor), Noggin, and a TGF-beta (transforming growth factor beta) inhibitor. Tumour cells often carry mutations that switch those same pathways on permanently, so they no longer need them supplied. Remove a growth factor from the medium and the normal cells die while the mutant ones carry on.

The colorectal example is the clean one. Biallelic inactivation of the adenomatous polyposis coli (APC) gene occurs in more than 80 per cent of colorectal cancers and switches the Wnt pathway on permanently, so those tumour cells grow in medium with no Wnt or R-spondin at all, while normal colonic cells die rapidly. Zhang and He report that this selective conditioning achieves more than 95 per cent tumour cell purity in most cases, with no manual dissection and no cell sorting required.

Think of it like this

It is the trick of finding who in a room speaks a language by turning off the subtitles. You do not have to ask anybody. The ones who cannot follow leave.

You do not pick the tumour cells out. You change the conditions so only they can live.

Where the sample comes from, and how often it works

Three sample routes, with very different success rates and very different clinical meanings. Surgical resection specimens are the commonest source, with establishment success often exceeding 80 per cent for colorectal, gastric, breast and pancreatic cancers, and the large advantage that you can grow paired normal tissue organoids from the same patient as a control. The limitation is brutal: only patients having surgery, which excludes most people with advanced, metastatic or unresectable disease, who are exactly the people who would benefit most.

Core needle biopsies close some of that gap. They yield only milligrams of tissue and still produce organoids at high success rates across breast, gastric, prostate and lung cancer, and because you can take them repeatedly, they allow organoids to be grown from the same patient before treatment and again at the point of resistance.

Liquid biopsy samples are the most minimally invasive of all: ascites, pleural effusion, urine, sputum, pancreatic juice, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and circulating tumour cells (CTCs) from blood. The last of those is the hardest, and the number is worth holding on to: CTCs (circulating tumour cells) can be as few as one cell per millilitre of blood. Organoids have still been grown from them in metastatic breast, colorectal and small cell lung cancer.

The easier the sample is to take, the harder the culture is. That trade runs through the whole field.

Does the dish still match the patient?

This is the question the whole enterprise rests on, and the review answers it at four levels. Histopathologically, tumour organoids reproduce glandular formation, nuclear pleomorphism, mitotic index and differentiation grade across nearly all solid tumour types.

Genomically, and this is the number to remember, multiple studies confirm that early passage tumour organoids maintain more than 90 per cent genomic concordance with the original primary tumour, preserving both clonal driver mutations and subclonal heterogeneity including rare subclones linked to resistance and early relapse. The core driver mutational profile stays stable for more than 20 passages in most lines, although long term culture can cause minor clonal selection.

Functionally, organoids form tumours in immunocompromised mice that match the original patient tumour, and their response to drugs in the dish correlates with the patient's clinical response. That last point is the one with consequences, and it gets its own slide.

Think of it like this

Ninety per cent genomic concordance sounds like a pass mark. Read it the other way: nearly one mutation in ten is different, and any of them could be the one that matters.

More than 90 per cent genomic concordance, stable past 20 passages. Good enough to reason with, not good enough to trust blindly.

Test yourself

Think about it: the purity trick and what it costs

  1. Selective media enrich for tumour cells by starving normal cells of a growth factor the tumour no longer needs. What kind of tumour cell would this method systematically lose?

    Any tumour cell that has not yet acquired the mutation being selected for. If you starve a colorectal culture of Wnt and R-spondin, you select for cells with the adenomatous polyposis coli (APC) pathway already switched on, and a subclone driven by something else is at a disadvantage. Zhang and He present the method as achieving more than 95 per cent purity and do not frame it as a source of bias, so treat this as a question worth asking rather than a criticism the paper makes.

  2. Organoid establishment succeeds in over 80 per cent of surgical samples but the patients who most need the result are the ones without surgery. What does that do to a published success rate?

    It makes the headline figure describe a population that is not the population of interest. The 80 per cent is measured in resectable disease; the patients with advanced, metastatic or unresectable cancer arrive through needle or liquid biopsy routes with their own, generally lower, success rates. This is a selection effect in the denominator, and it is the single most common way a technique looks better on paper than it will in the clinic.

What the dish is actually for

Rebuilding cancer from scratch, predicting a patient's treatment, and putting an immune system in the well.

Rebuilding colorectal cancer from scratch

In 1990 a model of colorectal cancer was proposed in which the disease develops through a specific sequence of driver mutations. It was built by looking at human tumour samples and inferring the order. It was not tested directly in a human system for twenty five years, because there was no human system to test it in.

Zhang and He describe the landmark 2015 studies from the Clevers group that finally did it. Normal human intestinal organoids were edited with CRISPR-Cas9 to introduce, one at a time, the four commonest colorectal driver mutations: adenomatous polyposis coli (APC), Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS), tumour protein p53 (TP53), and SMAD4. That was sufficient to transform them into invasive carcinoma, which then formed metastatic tumours when transplanted into mice.

The review calls this the first direct experimental validation of the multi-step model of colorectal carcinogenesis. Notice the shape of the argument: for twenty five years the evidence was correlational, a pattern read off static samples. The organoid turned it into an experiment.

Think of it like this

It is the difference between reading a crash report and driving the car into the wall on purpose. Only one of them tells you what caused what.

Four mutations, in order, in a dish, and normal gut becomes invasive cancer. The 1990 model, finally tested.

The living avatar, and the asymmetry in what it tells you

Genomic profiling is standard care for many advanced cancers, and yet only a minority of patients get clinical benefit from genomically guided therapy, because most drugs have no validated predictive biomarker and heterogeneity undermines the ones that do. The organoid answer is to stop predicting and start testing: grow the patient's tumour, expose it to a hundred regimens, see what kills it.

The review reports that ex vivo drug sensitivity testing in patient derived organoids correlates with clinical response across colorectal, gastric, breast, pancreatic, lung and ovarian cancer. But the two directions are not equally strong, and the difference is the practically important part. Organoid testing has a high negative predictive value: drugs that are inactive in the organoid are almost universally ineffective in the patient. It also has a high positive predictive value, but the review states the negative case in the stronger terms.

That asymmetry is worth more than it sounds. A test that reliably tells you what will not work lets you take a toxic, useless treatment off the table before the patient has to endure it, which is a real clinical benefit even if the test never once tells you the right answer.

Think of it like this

A metal detector that never misses buried metal is useful even if half of what it finds is bottle tops. The value is in clearing the ground you can rule out.

Its greatest strength is telling you what will not work. That is not a consolation prize.

Putting an immune system in the well

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting the programmed cell death protein 1 and its ligand (PD-1 and PD-L1), and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), have transformed treatment for some patients. Most patients with solid tumours do not respond, and progress has been held up by a modelling problem that is close to absolute: the two standard preclinical models are structurally incapable of studying this. Two dimensional cell lines have no immune system, and mouse xenograft models require an immunodeficient host by definition.

The organoid answer is co-culture. Tumour organoids are grown together with the same patient's own immune cells, and the landmark studies showed that these co-cultures generate tumour-reactive T cells that specifically kill the tumour organoids while sparing normal tissue organoids from the same patient. That last clause is the control that makes it convincing.

The clinical result is the striking one. Zhang and He report that organoid-based immune reactivity assays predict clinical response to anti-PD-1 and anti-PD-L1 therapy with an accuracy of more than 85 per cent, exceeding the predictive performance of conventional genomic biomarkers such as PD-L1 (programmed cell death ligand 1) expression, tumour mutational burden (TMB) and microsatellite instability (MSI) status.

Think of it like this

The existing biomarkers ask whether a tumour looks like the sort of thing an immune system attacks. The co-culture asks this immune system, about this tumour, and watches.

A functional test beat the genomic biomarkers. Watching beats predicting.
