The Body Clock, Aging and Cancer

Three fields that turned out to share the same machinery.

Built from Wang J. et al. (2025). Research 8:0612. CC BY 4.0.

Read Wang et al. 2025, the source paper

Before you start

This course is built from one paper: Wang and colleagues, published in Research in 2025, volume 8, article 0612. It is Open Access under a Creative Commons Attribution licence, and the full text was fetched from Europe PMC rather than read second hand.

It follows directly from the Hallmarks of Cancer deck and assumes you have worked through it. Where this paper uses a hallmark by name, the definition is the one Hanahan gives. What is new here is a third axis that the hallmarks framework barely touches: time of day.

One warning about the source, and it matters more here than it did for Hanahan. This is a review by a large group, published in a good but not flagship journal, and it synthesises a field that is still young. Several of its central claims rest on single studies or on mouse work. The deck flags those as they arise, and you should treat them as leads rather than settled facts.

A map of an unfinished field, which is exactly why there is room in it.

Quotation
As long as one lives long enough, developing cancer becomes an inevitable occurrence.

Wang et al. 2025, Introduction, paraphrasing what the epidemiology implies. The cumulative risk of cancer by age 75 is 21.4 per cent, and latent prostate cancer found at autopsy runs from 35.1 to 51 per cent.

Why put these three subjects in one room

Aging is the single largest risk factor for cancer, and the relationship is not subtle. Between 1980 and 2000 the population of the United States grew by 23 per cent, from 227 million to 279 million, while annual cancer diagnoses rose by 66 per cent, from 807,000 to 1.34 million. Almost all of that increase came from patients aged 65 and over.

Circadian rhythm disruption is separately linked to cancer. In 2007 the International Agency for Research on Cancer, part of the World Health Organization, classified circadian disruption from shift work as a probable human carcinogen, Group 2A. That is the same category as red meat and hot beverages above 65 degrees, which is worth knowing so you neither overstate nor dismiss it.

And the two interact. Aging degrades the clock: rhythms weaken, sleep fragments, and the body's timing signals lose amplitude. Meanwhile a broken clock accelerates aging, through oxidative stress and inflammation. So you have three processes, each of which worsens the other two, and until recently they were studied by three sets of people who did not talk to each other.

Think of it like this

Three doctors examining the same patient through three different doors, each convinced they are treating a different illness.

Part one: how a clock is built from genes

The mechanism everything else depends on.

What makes something a circadian clock

Three properties have to hold together, and a system missing any one of them is not a circadian clock. First, oscillation: gene expression or protein levels must rise and fall on a cycle of roughly 24 hours. Second, entrainment: that cycle must be resettable by an external cue, called a zeitgeber, German for time-giver. Light is the main one, but temperature and feeding also work.

Third, and least obvious, free-running. Remove the external cue entirely, put the organism in constant darkness, and the rhythm keeps going anyway. That is what separates a genuine internal clock from something merely reacting to sunrise. The clock is generated inside the cell, and light only corrects its drift.

The distinction matters clinically. If rhythms were simply a response to daylight, you could fix a disrupted patient by controlling their light exposure. Because they are internally generated, a clock that has been chronically pushed out of alignment can stay wrong even after the environment is fixed.

Think of it like this

A wristwatch keeps time by itself and you correct it against the station clock now and then. It does not read the station clock continuously.

Oscillation, entrainment, free-running. All three, or it is not a clock.

The engine: a protein that switches off its own gene

The loop, in words

During the day, two proteins called CLOCK (circadian locomotor output cycles kaput) and BMAL1 (brain and muscle ARNT-like 1) join together as a heterodimer, meaning a pair of two different proteins. That pair binds a short DNA sequence, CACGTG, known as an E-box, sitting in the promoters of two gene families: PER (period) and CRY (cryptochrome). Binding switches those genes on.

PER and CRY proteins are then made and accumulate in the cytoplasm through the afternoon and evening. As night comes, they move back into the nucleus, pair up, and bind CLOCK and BMAL1, shutting them down. In doing so they switch off the very genes that produced them.

With production halted, existing PER and CRY are gradually destroyed. Casein kinase adds phosphate groups that mark them for ubiquitination and destruction by the proteasome. As they disappear the repression lifts, CLOCK and BMAL1 are free again, and the cycle restarts. The whole circuit takes about 24 hours, and the delay is not incidental: the time taken to make, move, and destroy these proteins is what sets the period.

Think of it like this

A thermostat that takes a whole day to notice the room is warm. The sluggishness is the design, not a fault.

The period of the clock is the time it takes to build and dismantle a protein.

The second loop, and where the clock lives

A single feedback loop would be fragile, so there is a second one stabilising it. Nuclear receptors NR1D1 and NR1D2 (nuclear receptor subfamily 1 group D) repress BMAL1, while RORα, RORβ and RORγ (retinoic acid-related orphan receptors) activate it, both acting through response elements in the BMAL1 promoter. CLOCK and BMAL1 in turn drive NR1D transcription, so the two loops are coupled.

Anatomically, the master clock sits in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), a small region of the hypothalamus that receives light information from the eyes. But the SCN is not the only clock. Nearly every peripheral organ, including liver, kidney, lung, pancreas, stomach, adrenal gland, bladder and prostate, runs its own version of the same loop.

The SCN's job is to keep those peripheral clocks in step with each other and with the outside world. When that coordination fails, tissues start keeping different times from one another, which is a better description of what shift work does to you than simply saying you are tired.

Think of it like this

An orchestra where every section can play in time on its own, and the conductor exists only to stop them drifting apart.

Test yourself

Think about it: what the free-running property implies

  1. A patient has done rotating night shifts for fifteen years and then moves to a permanent day job. On the light-response model, how long should recovery take? On the free-running model?

    If rhythms were purely a response to light, recovery would be near immediate once the light schedule normalises. Because the clock is internally generated and coupled across many peripheral organs, realignment is slow and may be incomplete, and different organs may resynchronise at different rates. The second model predicts lasting risk after exposure ends, which is a testable and important difference.

  2. Why does the existence of independent peripheral clocks make the whole system more vulnerable rather than more robust?

    Because there are now many things to keep in phase rather than one. Redundancy protects against a clock stopping, but it creates a new failure mode that a single clock could not have: internal desynchrony, where the liver and the brain disagree about what time it is.

The paper notes a study where the skin clock could override the brain's.

Part two: the clock and cancer

How a timing system ends up controlling tumour biology.

The cell cycle already runs on a schedule

Cell division is not evenly distributed across the day, and once you know that, the connection to cancer stops being surprising. DNA replication in many organisms is concentrated at night, and one long-standing suggestion is that this evolved to keep replication away from daytime ultraviolet exposure, when newly opened DNA would be most vulnerable.

The machinery is directly clock-controlled. c-MYC and cyclin D1, both needed for entry into the DNA synthesis phase, are transcribed under CLOCK and BMAL1 control and therefore rise and fall daily. Knocking down BMAL1 or CLOCK reduces Wee1, which triggers apoptosis and raises p21, arresting cells at the G2 to M transition. Other checkpoint regulators, including p16INK4a, p27, p57 and the CDK1 and cyclin B1 pair, are similarly rhythmic.

Read that list again with the hallmarks in mind. Sustaining proliferative signalling, the first hallmark of cancer, is partly a question of what time it is. A tumour with a broken clock has lost a brake that healthy tissue still has.

Proliferation is not just controlled. It is scheduled.

p53 and the clock regulate each other

TP53 (tumour protein p53) is the most mutated gene in human cancer, and you met it repeatedly in the hallmarks deck. What is new here is that the clock and p53 are wired into each other in both directions.

Going one way: BMAL1 and PER2 control p53's transcription, stability and activity. Losing BMAL1 blocks p53 from inducing p21. Losing PER2 impairs p53's response to DNA damage. The mechanism is neat: MDM2 (mouse double minute 2 homolog) is the enzyme that normally tags p53 for destruction, and PER2 binds MDM2 and shields p53 from it. More PER2 means more p53 survives.

Going the other way: p53 blocks CLOCK and BMAL1 from binding the PER2 promoter, suppressing PER2 expression. So the clock protects p53, and p53 restrains the clock. Two of the most important control systems in the cell are in a mutual embrace, and damaging either one perturbs the other.

Think of it like this

Two people each holding the other's safety rope. It works beautifully until one of them slips.

You cannot break the clock without touching p53, or break p53 without touching the clock.

DNA repair is scheduled too

There are several repair systems: direct repair, base excision repair, nucleotide excision repair, double-strand break repair and cross-link repair. The paper's claim is that circadian rhythms regulate all of them, which is a strong statement worth holding lightly, but the specific mechanisms it cites are concrete.

Nucleotide excision repair, which fixes a broad range of damaged bases, depends on a factor called XPA. XPA is switched on by CLOCK and BMAL1 and switched off by CRY and PER, so repair capacity itself oscillates through the day. Double-strand break repair is also clock-controlled, through a BMAL1, CLOCK and histone H4 acetylation axis that promotes the DNA end resection needed for homologous recombination.

The therapeutic consequence is immediate and testable. Depleting BMAL1 makes adrenocortical carcinoma markedly more sensitive to DNA-damaging treatment. If a tumour's ability to repair itself rises and falls on a daily cycle, then the hour at which you deliver radiotherapy or a DNA-damaging drug is not a logistical detail.

If repair capacity has a daily peak, giving damage at the trough is free efficacy.
