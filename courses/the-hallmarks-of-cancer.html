The Hallmarks of Cancer

What every cancer must learn to do, and how the framework was built.

Before you start

This course is built from a single paper: Douglas Hanahan's 2026 review in Cell, volume 189, pages 2254 to 2277. Every percentage and gene name you will read here appears in that paper, which is linked from this course and is free to read in full.

Where the deck adds something of its own, whether an analogy, a piece of exam technique or a judgement about what matters, it says so on the slide. You should always be able to tell Hanahan's voice from this course's, because in a seminar you will be held to the difference.

One warning about the source. Hanahan calls it "a concise synopsis, not a comprehensive review but rather a strategic overview". It is a map, not the territory. For any mechanism you intend to rely on in your own work, follow his citation to the focused review he points at, and from there to the primary papers underneath.

Read this as an orientation to the field, never as the evidence itself.

Quotation
Cancer presents a remarkably instructive perturbation of mechanisms manifesting in our biology that have gone awry.

Hanahan 2026, opening line of the Summary.

Why the field needed a framework at all

By the late 1990s cancer research had a volume problem rather than a knowledge problem. Thousands of laboratories were producing findings across genetics, cell biology, tissue biology, pathology and clinical medicine, and no single person could hold the literature in their head. Worse, the findings looked unrelated. A mutation in a colon tumour and a change in blood vessel growth in a melanoma seemed to belong to different subjects entirely.

Hanahan and Weinberg's move in 2000 was to stop asking what is different between cancers and start asking what every cancer must nevertheless manage to do. Their aim, in their own words, was to "organize and reconcile the vast diversity of genetic and phenotypic characteristics observed in human cancers". The answer they proposed was a short list of capabilities that any successful tumour has to acquire, however it happens to acquire them.

Notice what kind of claim that is. It is not a law of nature and Hanahan has never claimed it is one. It is a heuristic, which means a simplifying tool that earns its place by being useful rather than by being true. Holding that distinction clearly in mind is the difference between a competent answer on this topic and a good one.

Think of it like this

It works like the periodic table before anyone understood the atom. The table organised the elements usefully and predicted gaps, long before it could explain why any of it was so.

A framework earns its place by being useful, not by being true.

Dimension one: the nine hallmarks

Acquired functional capabilities. What a cancer must be able to do.

What the word hallmark is actually doing

Hanahan sets two conditions. A hallmark must be distinctive in its mechanistic operations, meaning it is separable from the other eight, and it must be necessary, to varying degrees, in most forms of human cancer. Read that second condition slowly. It says most, not all, and it says varying degrees. The hedges are deliberate and they are load bearing.

The crucial word, though, is acquired. A hallmark is something the tumour gains during its development that the normal cell it came from could not do. And it is a capability, not a mutation. That distinction sounds pedantic until you meet a tumour with no driver mutation at all, at which point it becomes the reason the whole framework survives.

So when you are asked to discuss a hallmark, you are being asked about something a tumour can do, not about a gene it carries. Genes are how the capability tends to be acquired. They are not the capability itself.

Think of it like this

Think of a driving licence rather than a car. The capability is being able to drive; the car is merely one way of exercising it. Take the car away and the licence still exists.

A hallmark is a verb, not a noun. Something the tumour does, not something it has.

Diagram

The nine, in two groups

One. Sustaining proliferative signalling

A healthy cell divides only when it is told to, and the instruction is always temporary. Growth factors arrive, a receptor fires, a signalling cascade runs, the cell divides, and then the signal decays and everything goes quiet again. The whole system is built around the assumption that proliferation is an exception rather than a default.

Cancer's first trick is to jam that signal permanently on. The commonest way is a mutation in KRAS (Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog), a small protein that relays growth instructions inwards from the cell surface. Mutated KRAS behaves as though a growth factor were permanently present. It is altered in roughly thirty per cent of all human tumours, and the concentration in particular tissues is striking: ninety per cent of pancreatic adenocarcinomas, fifty per cent of colorectal cancers, thirty-five per cent of lung adenocarcinomas.

The other giant is MYC (myelocytomatosis oncogene), a transcription factor, which is a protein controlling which genes get read. MYC governs thousands of genes at once, and it is amplified, rearranged, or has its antagonists deleted in forty per cent of human tumours. Altogether COSMIC (Catalogue Of Somatic Mutations In Cancer) lists 193 genes validated as proliferation-driving oncogenes.

Think of it like this

A normal growth signal is a doorbell: someone presses it, you answer, it stops. Mutant KRAS is the doorbell jammed with a matchstick.

The capability is chronic proliferation. The oncogene is only the matchstick.

Test yourself

Think about it: the exception that shapes the rule

  1. A small number of cancers, notably some childhood ones, carry no recurrently mutated or amplified driver oncogene at all. Does that break the claim that this hallmark is necessary?

    No, and understanding why is the point. The capability is still there; it is acquired by two routes that involve no driver mutation. One is epigenetic reprogramming, which changes which genes the cell reads without altering the sequence. The other is autocrine and paracrine growth factor supply, meaning the cell feeds growth signals to itself or receives them from neighbours.

  2. Why is that worth defending rather than glossing over?

    Because a framework built on mutations would have been falsified by these tumours, and one built on capabilities absorbs them instead. Whether you call that admirable robustness or convenient unfalsifiability is a genuine argument, and you can hold either position credibly.

Best answers name the capability-versus-mutation distinction explicitly.

Two. Inactivating growth suppressors

If the first hallmark is jamming the accelerator, the second is cutting the brake cables, and the cell has a great many brakes. A family of proteins sits at each checkpoint of the division cycle asking whether the cell really ought to proceed. RB (retinoblastoma protein) blocks the transcriptional programme that division requires. APC (adenomatous polyposis coli) keeps a proliferation-promoting signal suppressed. A set of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors, encoded by CDKN1A, CDKN1B and CDKN2A, hold the cycle at its gates.

Above all of them sits TP53 (tumour protein p53), which is less a brake than a control room. It senses damage, oncogenic signalling and low oxygen, and in response it can arrest the cycle, force the cell into permanent retirement, or order its suicide. Losing it removes not one safeguard but a whole tier of them, which is why it is mutated in about forty per cent of cancers examined by whole-genome sequencing. Sixty to seventy per cent of those changes are missense mutations clustered at a handful of hotspots.

There is also a quieter half of this hallmark that most summaries skip. Growth suppression is not only internal. Neighbouring cells restrain each other, and cells compete for limited nutrients and oxygen so that expansion means outcompeting the tissue around you. Hanahan calls these paracrine mechanisms less well defined, which is a polite way of saying nobody has nailed them down yet.

Think of it like this

TP53 is not a brake pedal. It is the control room that decides whether to brake, stop the train permanently, or evacuate it.

A named gap in the literature, like paracrine growth suppression, is a research question waiting for someone to take it.

Three. Resisting programmed cell death

Multicellular life depends on cells being willing to die on command. A cell that detects irreparable damage in itself is supposed to dismantle itself quietly, and the machinery for doing so is pre-installed in every one of us. Apoptosis is the best understood version: a cascade of proteases called caspases that take the cell apart in an orderly way.

The decision is made at the mitochondrion, and it is a tug of war. On one side, BH3-only proteins including PUMA, BIM and NOXA push towards death. On the other, anti-apoptotic gatekeepers BCL-2, BCL-XL and MCL-1 hold them back by binding them up. Tip the balance towards the gatekeepers, by making more of them or by deleting TP53 (tumour protein p53) so that PUMA and NOXA are never switched on, and the cell survives damage it should not have survived.

Apoptosis is not the only route, and the alternatives matter clinically. Necroptosis, ferroptosis, pyroptosis and certain forms of autophagy all kill cells through partly overlapping machinery. Where this hallmark has genuinely delivered treatment is venetoclax, a BCL-2 inhibitor approved for chronic lymphocytic leukaemia and acute myeloid leukaemia. Beyond those, Hanahan is blunt that the benefit across cancers generally is "unclear and under continuing investigation".

Think of it like this

Every cell carries a self-destruct switch with two hands on it. Cancer does not disable the switch; it just makes sure the hand holding it back is stronger.

Venetoclax works because it prises the gatekeeper's fingers off the switch.

Test yourself

Think about it: apoptosis as an indirect oncogene

  1. Dying cancer cells can release signals that help their surviving neighbours, and some cells escape apoptosis part-way through with damaged but still viable genomes carrying new mutations. What does that do to the idea that cell death is straightforwardly protective?

    It makes the barrier conditional rather than absolute. Killing cells is not automatically good for the patient, and a therapy that kills inefficiently could in principle select for exactly the cells that survive the attempt. Hanahan presents these as case studies, so weigh them as such.

  2. How would you test whether signals from dying cells actually accelerate tumour growth in a living animal?

    The hard part is separating the killing from the signalling, since both happen together. You would need to compare cells dying with functional caspases against cells dying without them, while holding the total number of deaths constant. If growth differs, the signal rather than the loss is doing the work.
