Start here, and read this bit properly

This is the course to do first. The other two courses in this series both assume you already know what a mutation is, what a gene does and why a cell dividing when it should not is a problem. This one assumes none of that.

It is built on one paper, a 2026 review by Kenichi Yoshida in Experimental and Molecular Medicine, volume 58, pages 961 to 969, on what happens in normal tissue before a cancer ever exists. Figures for how common cancer is come from the World Health Organization (WHO) cancer fact sheet. Both are linked from this course and both are free to read.

Where a slide states something that is simply standard terminology, the kind of definition that is the same in every textbook, it carries no citation and says so. Where it is this course's own analogy or judgement, it says that too. You should always be able to tell whose voice you are reading.

Think of it like this

Think of it as learning the rules of a sport before watching a match. Nobody enjoys the match more for having the rules explained during it.

Do this course first. The other two are written for someone who has already done it.

The picture almost everyone starts with

Ask most people what cancer is and you get a version of this. A cell goes wrong. It starts dividing when it should not. It builds up into a lump. The lump spreads. That picture is not false, and it will get you through a conversation, but it is missing the part that makes the disease so hard to catch early and so hard to explain.

The missing part is that the first three steps happen constantly, in everybody, without producing cancer. Cells do go wrong. They do divide when they should not. Clusters of them do build up. If that were sufficient, every adult would be riddled with tumours by forty. Something else has to be true before any of it becomes a disease.

So the useful question is not what makes a cell go wrong. It is why, given how routinely cells go wrong, cancer is as rare as it is. Turn the question around like that and the rest of the subject falls into an order that actually makes sense.

Think of it like this

It is like asking why houses burn down. Sparks are everywhere, all the time, in every kitchen and every plug socket. The interesting question was never where the spark came from. It is what had to be true about the house for the spark to catch.

The mystery is not that cancer happens. It is that it hardly ever does.

Eleven words to learn first

The rest of the course is written using these and nothing beyond them.

Glossary

The eleven words you cannot skip

Standard terminology, not from any one paper. These definitions are the same in every textbook, so they carry no citation.

Cell
One unit of a living body, walled off from its neighbours. You are built from tens of trillions of them.
DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid)
The long chemical chain inside a cell that carries its instructions, written in an alphabet of four letters called bases.
Genome
The whole set of DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) in one cell. Every cell in your body carries a copy of the same one.
Gene
One stretch of that DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) holding the instructions for one job, usually the recipe for one protein.
Protein
The machine a gene's recipe builds. Proteins do the actual work: they hold the cell together, read signals and decide when to divide.
Mutation
A change in the DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) letters. Most are harmless. A few change what a protein does.
Somatic mutation
A mutation that happens in an ordinary body cell during your life. It is not inherited and you cannot pass it on.
Germline mutation
A mutation you were born with, present in every cell including egg or sperm, and therefore inheritable.
Clone
All the cells descended from one single cell. They carry that cell's mutations because they are copies of it.
Driver mutation
A mutation that changes the cell's behaviour in a way that gives it an advantage over its neighbours. Most mutations are not one of these. A whole section below is about why.
Clonal evolution
The process by which clones carrying different mutations compete over time, with the ones that have an advantage taking up more of the tissue. It is natural selection, running inside one body.

Why the word clone is the important one

Clone is the word that does the heavy lifting for the rest of this course, and it is worth being precise about. A clone is not a copy of a person and it is nothing to do with cloning sheep. It just means every cell that descended from one particular starting cell.

That matters because of what it implies about mutations. If a cell picks up a mutation and then divides, both daughters carry it. If they divide, all four carry it. A mutation in a single cell is a curiosity. A mutation in a cell that then produces a million descendants is a patch of tissue where a million cells all share the same flaw, and that patch is a far bigger target for the next thing to go wrong.

So there are two separate questions, and confusing them is where most beginner explanations of cancer fall apart. First: did a cell acquire a mutation? Second: did that cell go on to take over a piece of tissue? The first happens constantly. The second is the one that counts.

Think of it like this

A typo in one printed copy of a book is a typo. The same typo in the master plate, after another hundred thousand copies have been run off it, is an edition.

Mutation is an event. A clone is a foothold. Only the second one builds towards anything.

Test yourself

Think about it: somatic and germline

Answer both before revealing. The second one catches people out more often than the first.

  1. A person inherits a faulty copy of a gene from a parent, so the fault is in every cell of their body from birth. Does that mean they have cancer?

    No, and the distinction is the whole point. They have a germline mutation, which is a starting condition, not a disease. What it changes is the odds: every cell in the body begins one step further along than everyone else's, so fewer further steps are needed. Yoshida gives a concrete version of this. In people carrying germline mutations in BRCA1 or BRCA2, normal breast cells that are not cancer already show a higher rate of whole chromosome abnormality, 3.63 to 3.65 per cent against 2.45 per cent in people without those mutations.

  2. Why can a somatic mutation never be inherited by your children, no matter how serious it is?

    Because your children are built from egg or sperm cells, and a mutation that happened in, say, a cell in your lung is not present in those. Inheritance only carries what is in the germline. This is why a lifetime of smoking damages the smoker's lung DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) without writing that damage into the next generation.

Everyone reading this is already full of mutations

The single finding that reorganises everything else, and it is not a finding about cancer at all.

The finding that should reset your intuition

Until recently it was genuinely difficult to look for mutations in normal tissue. A tumour gives you billions of cells that are all descendants of one cell, so its mutations are easy to read. A patch of healthy tissue is a jumble of thousands of unrelated small clones, and any mutation present in only a few cells is drowned out by the rest.

The technology that solved this is worth knowing about, because it explains why this is a recent story. One approach cuts out as few as 100 to 1000 cells with a laser and sequences those. Another grows a colony from one single cell and sequences the colony. A third, called duplex sequencing, reads both strands of the DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) and only believes a mutation if it appears on both, which drops the error rate low enough to read mutations in bulk normal tissue directly. One such method, nanorate sequencing, has fewer than five errors in every billion base pairs read, which Yoshida puts at fewer than 30 errors across an entire cell's genome.

What that machinery revealed is that somatic mutations accumulate in normal cells in every organ of every healthy person, driven by age, by environment and by inflammation. Not in patients. In everyone.

Think of it like this

For decades this was like trying to hear one conversation in a stadium. The advance was not a better question, it was a microphone good enough to pick a single voice out of the crowd.

Normal tissue is not a clean background against which cancer stands out. It is already covered in writing.

Reading that chart properly

The first thing to take from it is the sheer scale. A cell lining your colon picks up something like 43.6 new single base substitutions (SBS), meaning single letter changes in the DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid), every year. Over sixty years that is thousands of changes, in tissue that is entirely normal. One caution about the chart before you read anything into it: Table 1 records mutation burden in normal tissue and says nothing about who the tissue came from, and for several organs the paper compares normal tissue with cancer in the same individual, so not every donor was cancer free.

The second thing is that the ordering is not what you would guess. The gut leads, which fits a tissue that is constantly renewing itself and constantly exposed to whatever you swallowed. But look at neurons at 17.1 per year and heart muscle at 19. Those cells barely divide. They are still accumulating mutations at close to half the rate of the colon, which tells you that a large part of this has nothing to do with cell division at all.

The third thing is what is missing. The bladder row in the source reads unknown, so it is not on the chart. That is worth pausing on: the honest thing a chart can do is leave a gap where the data has a gap, and a chart that has a bar for everything is often a chart that invented one.

Think of it like this

It is closer to rust than to injury. Rust does not need anything to happen to you. It is what the material does when left in contact with the air.

Cells that never divide still mutate. Whatever is causing this, copying errors are only part of it.

What a mutational signature actually is

This is the one genuinely clever idea in the chapter and it is worth slowing down for. Different causes of DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) damage do not scatter mutations randomly. Each leaves a characteristic pattern: which letter changes into which, and which letters tend to sit either side. That pattern is called a mutational signature, and they are numbered, so SBS4 means single base substitution signature number four.

The consequence is remarkable. Given a piece of tissue, you can read the mutations and work backwards to what caused them. Signature SBS4 (single base substitution signature 4) is tobacco smoke, and experiments in stem cells point at benzo[a]pyrene, one of the carcinogens in it, as the agent. SBS7 (single base substitution signature 7) is ultraviolet (UV) light. SBS22 (single base substitution signature 22) is aristolochic acid, from plants of the genus Aristolochia. SBS24 (single base substitution signature 24) is aflatoxin, which the World Health Organization (WHO) describes as a food contaminant. It is a toxin produced by moulds, not itself a mould.

Note carefully what a signature is and is not. It is the fingerprint the cause leaves behind, not the damage and not the disease. Finding the smoking signature in someone's lung tells you tobacco smoke reached those cells. It does not by itself tell you they will get cancer.

Think of it like this

It works like tool marks in forensics. A jemmy, a screwdriver and a bolt cutter each leave a different edge on the same broken lock, so the mark tells you which tool was used long after whoever used it has gone.

A mutational signature is evidence of exposure. It is not a diagnosis.

Diagram

Two kinds of cause

The clock that runs whatever you do

Two of the signatures behave differently from the rest, and they are the ones that matter most for understanding why age is the dominant risk factor in cancer. SBS1 (single base substitution signature 1) and SBS5 (single base substitution signature 5), together with SBS40 (single base substitution signature 40) which looks so similar to SBS5 (single base substitution signature 5) that they may be the same thing, are described as clock-like, because the number of mutations they produce tracks with a person's age.

SBS1 (single base substitution signature 1) has a known mechanism. A chemical tag called a methyl group sits on many of the cytosine bases in your DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid), and that tagged base spontaneously falls apart into something the cell reads as a different letter. Nothing has to happen to you for this. It is chemistry proceeding at body temperature. SBS5 (single base substitution signature 5) and SBS40 (single base substitution signature 40) also rise steadily with age and their cause is, in the paper's own account, still unknown.

The reason this reframes everything is that it separates two things usually run together. Some of your mutational burden is the consequence of choices and exposures. Some of it is the consequence of having been alive, and no behaviour removes it.

Think of it like this

It is the difference between the miles on a car and the age of the rubber in its hoses. One is a record of how it was driven. The other happens on the driveway.

Age is not a proxy for a hard life. It is its own mutational process, with its own signature.

The result nobody guesses

One finding in this paper is worth the price of admission on its own, and it is a good test of whether the clock idea has landed. In women, normal breast tissue accumulates about 19.5 mutations per year before menopause. After menopause that drops to about 8.1 per year.

There is more. Yoshida reports that a single pregnancy is associated with a reduction of 54.8 mutations. The explanation offered is that both effects come from reduced cell division, as the menstrual cycle stops and oestrogen levels fall.

Be careful about what this does and does not license you to say. It is a measurement of mutation counts in normal breast tissue. It is not a statement about anyone's cancer risk, and the paper does not make it one. The reason to include it here is that it shows the mutation rate is not a fixed property of a tissue. It responds to what the body is doing.

Think of it like this

Like a factory's wear rate falling when it moves from three shifts to one. Less running, less wear. The machinery did not change.

Mutation rates are not constants. They move with the biology underneath them.

The sentence this whole course is built around

Normal cells undergo clonal evolution into cancer cells over several decades, with the initial acquisition of a driver mutation occurring in early life.

Yoshida 2026, abstract. Read it twice. Both halves of that sentence are surprising, and the second one is the part almost nobody expects.

Test yourself

Think about it: what the mutation clock explains

  1. Cancer incidence rises steeply with age. Before you knew any of this, what would you have assumed the reason was?

    Most people assume it is accumulated exposure: decades of smoke, sun, drink and pollution adding up. The WHO (World Health Organization) fact sheet gives that as part of it, alongside repair mechanisms becoming less effective with age. What the signature work adds is a third component that neither of those covers. The clock-like signatures SBS1 (single base substitution signature 1) and SBS5 (single base substitution signature 5) accumulate with age in every tissue including ones that never divide and are never exposed to anything. Part of the risk is simply elapsed time.

  2. If mutations accumulate in neurons and heart muscle at 17.1 and 19 per year, why are brain and heart cancers derived from those cells so much rarer than colon cancer?

    This is the right question and the honest answer is that mutation count is clearly not what decides it. The next section gives one large part of the reason, which is that a mutation only matters if the cell carrying it can expand into a clone, and cells that do not divide cannot. Hold onto the question, because it is the hinge the rest of the course turns on.

Why a mutation is not a cancer

The step almost every popular account of cancer skips, and the reason the disease is so much rarer than the damage.

Driver and passenger

Of the thousands of mutations a cell accumulates, the overwhelming majority land somewhere that makes no difference. They sit in stretches of DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) that do not code for anything, or they change a protein in a way that does not change what it does. These are called passenger mutations. They are carried along, and they do nothing.

A small minority land in a gene where the change alters the cell's behaviour in a way that gives it an advantage over its neighbours: it grows a little faster, or survives a little better, or ignores a signal telling it to stop. These are called driver mutations, and the name is exact. They drive.

The genes this happens in are not random either. Yoshida lists the ones that turn up repeatedly in specific normal tissues: NOTCH1, TP53 and FAT1 in the oesophagus and the airways, PIK3CA in the breast, KRAS and PIK3CA in the lining of the womb, ARID1A and KDM6A in the stomach, DNMT3A and TET2 in blood. The same short list keeps appearing, which is itself evidence that these are genuinely advantageous rather than accidental.

Think of it like this

On a crowded train, most passengers are just travelling. A driver mutation is a passenger who gets into the cab. Same train, entirely different consequence.

Passenger mutations are the noise. Driver mutations are the selection pressure.

The trap that this sets

Here is where it gets genuinely counterintuitive, and this is the part that separates a real understanding of cancer from a headline one. If driver mutations are the dangerous ones, you would expect them to be rare in healthy people. They are not.

In normal skin, mutations in NOTCH1 are found in roughly 20 per cent of cells, and the clones carrying them are physically larger than the clones without a driver, which is exactly what you would predict if the mutation confers an advantage. In the oesophagus the same NOTCH1 clones are found from infancy onwards, including in young healthy people with no smoking or drinking history at all. In the lining of the womb, mutations in KRAS and PIK3CA, both of which are common in womb cancer, are acquired before the age of ten.

So the tidy story, that a driver mutation appears and a cancer follows, is wrong. Driver mutations are ordinary. Most people are walking around with tissue that is substantially made of driver-mutant clones and will never develop a cancer in it.

Think of it like this

It is like discovering that a fifth of the drivers on the motorway have no licence, and the accident rate has not moved. Whatever prevents crashes, it was not the licensing.

Driver mutations are common. Cancer is rare. Any explanation has to survive that sentence.

Diagram

Why the tissue's shape decides the outcome

The colon, which should be the worst and is not

Look back at the mutation chart and the colon is near the very top, accumulating around 43.6 single base substitutions (SBS) per cell per year, with only the small intestine reaching higher. Now put that next to this: the frequency of driver mutations in normal colon lining is only about 1 per cent. One of the fastest mutating tissues in the body has almost none of the mutant clones that the slower ones are full of.

The reason Yoshida gives is architecture. The colon lining is built from separate tiny pits called crypts, and each crypt originates from a single stem cell. When a cell in a crypt picks up a driver mutation, there is nowhere for its descendants to go: the mutation would have to disrupt the crypt structure itself to spread, and it rarely does. The prostate works the same way, with the cells derived from its stem cells physically separated into distinct areas, and clonal expansion there is likewise rare. In the liver, the frequency of clones carrying driver mutations is under 5 per cent.

Skin and the oesophagus behave in the opposite way, and here the source and this course have to be separated carefully. What Yoshida reports is the observation: in epithelial tissues, clonal expansion of cells that have acquired driver mutations is frequently seen with age, and in skin the NOTCH1 clones reach 20 per cent of cells. He gives an architectural explanation only for the restrictive side, the colon, prostate and liver. The obvious reading, that a sheet of cells with nothing separating them lets an advantaged cell spread sideways, is this course's inference and not something the paper states.

Think of it like this

Compare a stadium of numbered seats with an open field. A pushier person in the field ends up with more space. In the stadium they end up with exactly one seat, however pushy they are. That is this course's picture of it, not the paper's.

Whether a mutation matters is decided partly by the mutation and partly by the shape of the tissue it landed in.

The version of this in your blood, which is different again

Blood is the exception that proves the point, because blood has no architecture at all. It is a liquid tissue circulating everywhere, so a blood stem cell with an advantage has nothing to push against. Its descendants simply become a larger and larger share of the blood.

This has a name, clonal haematopoiesis, and its prevalence is startling. Highly sensitive analyses find it in nearly every individual older than 75. Not a subset. Nearly everyone. Where driver mutations are involved the genes are most commonly DNMT3A and TET2, the same driver genes found mutated in blood cancers.

One qualification belongs here rather than in a footnote, because leaving it out would turn a hedged finding into a stronger claim than the source makes. Yoshida notes in his summary that in clonal haematopoiesis, clonal expansion often occurs without driver mutation acquisition at all. So the near universal finding in older people is expanded clones, which is not the same statement as a driver mutation in everyone, and what is driving the rest is one of the things the paper says is still poorly understood.

And yet almost none of those people get leukaemia. The risk is genuinely raised: myeloid clonal haematopoiesis carries a hazard ratio (HR) of 7.0 for developing a myeloid tumour and lymphoid clonal haematopoiesis a hazard ratio (HR) of 4.2 for a lymphoid one, meaning the rate of new cases is several times higher in the affected group. But the absolute figure is what you need alongside it: an estimated 0.5 to 1 per cent of people with detectable clonal haematopoiesis develop a blood cancer each year.

Think of it like this

Sevenfold sounds enormous until you ask sevenfold of what. Seven times a small number is still a small number, and this is the single most common way health statistics get misread.

A hazard ratio without an absolute risk beside it is not information.

Test yourself

Think about it: why so few clones become cancers

  1. Given that driver-mutant clones are present in most adults, in most tissues, propose two different explanations for why cancer is still uncommon.

    The first is the one this section makes: tissue architecture physically limits how far a clone can expand, so most clones are capped long before they become dangerous. The second is that one driver mutation is not enough. Yoshida's timelines show cancers arriving only after further drivers accumulate on top of the first, which takes decades and mostly does not finish. Yoshida is explicit that the full reason remains unclear, so if you can defend a third explanation, you are in live territory rather than settled.

  2. A study finds NOTCH1 mutations are common in normal oesophagus but relatively uncommon in oesophageal cancer. What is odd about that, and what might it mean?

    It is odd because a driver of clonal expansion in normal tissue ought to be enriched in the cancer that tissue produces, and it is the wrong way round. Yoshida reports mouse work in which mutant Notch1 promotes proliferation of normal oesophageal cells while inhibiting proliferation of cancer cells, which he says suggests that mutant Notch1 clones may play a role in tumorigenesis suppression. He proposes no mechanism for how, and neither does this course. He flags that the role of such clones requires further elucidation, so treat this as an open question, not a result.

If you took away one idea from this section, take this: the presence of a driver mutation is a starting condition, not a prognosis.

Cancer takes most of a lifetime

The timescale is the part that changes how you think about screening, prevention and the phrase early detection.

Diagram

When the first mutation actually arrives

Reading the timeline

Take the blood example first because it is the cleanest. In myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN), a group of blood cancers, genomic analysis puts the average time from acquiring the initial driver mutation, such as JAK2 V617F, to the disease appearing at roughly 30 years. Those driver mutations are often acquired in early life, in some cases before birth. Note what the paper does not give: an age. It says early life, including the fetal state, and no more than that, which is why the diagram marks it with a circle rather than a bar.

Breast is similar in shape. A chromosomal rearrangement written der(1;16) is acquired somewhere between early puberty and late adolescence, and it then takes over ten years, with further driver mutations accumulating, before a cancer develops. Where clonal haematopoiesis does progress to a blood cancer, samples collected over time show the clone expanding gradually across five to ten years first.

Sit with what that means. In a substantial fraction of cancers, the first step happened while the person was a child, and everything since has been slow accumulation. The disease is not an event. It is the tail end of a process that has been running quietly for most of a life.

Think of it like this

It is less like a fire starting and more like subsidence. The ground moved decades ago. The crack in the wall is just when you finally noticed.

For many cancers, the beginning was in childhood. The diagnosis is the last event, not the first.

And it does not always start from one cell

There is a standard picture of how a cancer arises which is worth stating so you can watch it get complicated. One cell acquires the full set of driver mutations, becomes the founder, and the entire tumour is its descendants. That is the classical model and for many cancers it is right.

Yoshida reports a case that does not fit. Phylogenetic analysis, which reconstructs the family tree of cells from the mutations they share, was done on normal breast tissue, pre-cancerous lesions and cancer from the same person. Multiple independent cancers turned out to have arisen from a common non-cancerous clone carrying der(1;16). The paper describes this as a pattern different from the traditional model of cancers evolving from a single cancer founder.

What follows from that is this course's reasoning and not the paper's, so weigh it as such: Yoshida reports the finding and draws no practical conclusion from it. But if several tumours grew separately out of one large field of already abnormal normal tissue, then the field is a different object from the tumours, and it is invisible to anything that looks for tumours. Whether that changes what should be done about it is a clinical question this course does not touch.

Think of it like this

Weeding a lawn is not the same as dealing with the roots running under it. You can clear every visible weed and have made no difference to what comes up next spring.

This course's reading: sometimes the tumours are the symptom, and the abnormal field they grew from is the thing itself.

Test yourself

Think about it: what a thirty year runway implies

  1. If the first driver mutation is typically acquired decades before any cancer, how far back could the moment of detection be pushed in principle?

    It suggests the ceiling is far earlier than current practice, and also that the target would have to change completely. Detecting a tumour early means finding a small tumour. Detecting the process early would mean finding an expanding driver-mutant clone in tissue that is not yet abnormal in any other way. Yoshida frames the whole field this way, closing that these findings are expected to contribute to early detection and prevention. The obstacle is the one this course has already given you: those clones are present in almost everyone and almost never progress, so finding one tells you very little on its own.

  2. Yoshida notes an increase in clones carrying few tobacco-related mutations in normal bronchial tissue after someone stops smoking. Why is that interesting, and what does it not prove?

    It is interesting because it suggests the population of clones in a tissue is not fixed. Less damaged clones appear to repopulate the airway once the exposure stops, which would be a mechanism for why stopping helps rather than merely halting further harm. What it does not prove is any particular effect on an individual's cancer risk, and Yoshida explicitly says the mechanism of clonal evolution or turnover in response to environmental change remains unclear.

So what actually makes it a cancer

Where this course stops and the next one starts, plus the words you will meet on any report or in any paper.

The gap this course leaves open on purpose

Everything so far has been about what happens before there is a cancer: mutations arriving, clones expanding, decades passing. What it has not told you is what a clone has to acquire in order to stop being an overgrown patch of normal tissue and become a malignant tumour.

That question has a well known answer with a name, the hallmarks of cancer, and it is the subject of the next course in this series. The short version is that a cancer is defined by a set of capabilities it has to acquire, such as sustaining its own growth signals, evading the mechanisms that would kill it, persuading blood vessels to grow towards it, and invading tissue it does not belong in.

The reason to keep the two separate is that they answer genuinely different questions. This course asks how a population of cells gets into position. That one asks what a population of cells in position then has to become. Doing them in the wrong order is why the hallmark framework often feels like a list to memorise rather than an argument.

Think of it like this

This course is about how a plot of land ends up cleared, levelled and served by a road. The next one is about what then gets built on it.

Getting into position and becoming malignant are two different problems. Most explanations run them together.

Glossary

The words on a pathology report

Standard terminology, so no citation. The one worth memorising is the last pair: grade is what the cells look like, stage is how far they have got, and they are constantly confused.

Neoplasm
Any new abnormal growth of tissue. The neutral word, covering both harmless and dangerous growths.
Tumour
A lump formed by a neoplasm. Not a synonym for cancer: most tumours are not cancerous.
Benign
A growth that stays where it is. It can still cause harm by pressing on something, but it does not invade or spread.
Malignant
A growth that invades surrounding tissue and can spread. Malignant is what the word cancer means.
In situ
Abnormal cells that have not yet broken through the boundary layer beneath them. Latin for in place.
Invasive
Abnormal cells that have broken through that boundary into the tissue underneath.
Primary
The original site where the cancer began.
Metastasis
A growth in a second site, seeded from the primary by cells that travelled there.
Carcinoma
A cancer arising from epithelium, the lining tissue of skin, gut, lungs and glands. Most cancers are these.
Sarcoma
A cancer arising from connective tissue: bone, muscle, fat, cartilage.
Leukaemia
A cancer of blood-forming cells, circulating rather than forming a solid lump.
Lymphoma
A cancer of lymphocytes, a type of white blood cell, usually forming masses in lymph nodes.
Grade
How abnormal the cells look under a microscope. A description of the cells themselves.
Stage
How far the cancer has spread. A description of its extent in the body, not its appearance.
TNM (tumour, node, metastasis)
The standard staging system: size of the tumour, whether nearby lymph nodes are involved, whether it has metastasised.

What cancer is, in the words of the body that counts it

One defining feature of cancer is the rapid creation of abnormal cells that grow beyond their usual boundaries, and which can then invade adjoining parts of the body and spread to other organs; the latter process is referred to as metastasis. Widespread metastases are the primary cause of death from cancer.

World Health Organization cancer fact sheet, retrieved 2 September 2026. The second sentence is the one to hold onto: it is almost never the original tumour that kills.

Test yourself

Think about it: benign, malignant and the words in between

  1. A growth is described as benign. Give a reason it might still need removing urgently.

    Because benign describes behaviour, not consequence. A benign growth that stays exactly where it is can still be fatal if where it is happens to be inside the skull pressing on the brainstem, or obstructing the bowel, or sitting on a major vessel. Benign means it will not invade or metastasise. It does not mean harmless.

  2. Why is the distinction between in situ and invasive the single most consequential line in this vocabulary?

    Because it is the point at which a cancer gains access to the routes out. In situ, the abnormal cells are still sitting on top of the boundary layer beneath the epithelium, with no blood or lymphatic vessels on their side of it. Once through it, they are in tissue that has both, and metastasis becomes possible. Given the WHO (World Health Organization) statement that widespread metastases are the primary cause of death from cancer, that boundary is the difference between a local problem and a lethal one.

How common, and how much of it is avoidable

Figures from the World Health Organization (WHO), with the caveats that belong next to them.

The scale of it

The World Health Organization (WHO) fact sheet records cancer as a leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for nearly 10 million deaths in 2024, or nearly one in six deaths from any cause.

The most common cancers in 2024 by number of new cases were lung at 2.6 million, breast at 2.4 million, colon and rectum at 2.0 million, prostate at 1.5 million, non-melanoma skin at 1.1 million and stomach at 1.0 million. The most common causes of cancer death that year were lung at 1.86 million, colon and rectum at 918,000, liver at 732,000, breast at 694,000 and stomach at 642,000.

Two things are worth noticing in those two lists rather than reading past them. First, they are not in the same order: breast is second by cases and fourth by deaths, while liver is third by deaths and does not appear in the case list at all. That gap is survival. Second, the picture is not uniform across the world. The WHO (World Health Organization) notes that cervical cancer is the most common cancer among women in 26 countries, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South and Central America, Melanesia and South East Asia.

Think of it like this

Comparing the two lists is like comparing how many people catch a train with how many miss their connection. The difference between the lists is the whole story.

Cases and deaths are different rankings. Where they diverge, they are telling you about survival.

How much of it is preventable, honestly stated

The World Health Organization (WHO) puts it at approximately 38 per cent of cancers currently preventable by avoiding risk factors and applying existing evidence-based prevention. That number deserves to be read in both directions.

Read one way it is enormous: nearly two in five, without a single new discovery. The named contributors are tobacco use, alcohol, unhealthy diet, physical inactivity and air pollution, with WHO (World Health Organization) attributing nearly a quarter of cancer deaths to tobacco use, alcohol consumption, high body mass index, low fruit and vegetable intake and lack of physical activity together. Chronic infections add more: approximately 10 per cent of cancers diagnosed globally in 2022 were attributed to carcinogenic infections including Helicobacter pylori, human papillomavirus (HPV), hepatitis B virus, hepatitis C virus and Epstein-Barr virus. Two of those, HPV (human papillomavirus) and hepatitis B, are vaccine preventable.

Read the other way, roughly three in five are not currently preventable, and everything earlier in this course explains why. If a substantial part of your mutational burden comes from clock-like processes that run whatever you do, then a substantial part of cancer risk is not a behaviour and cannot be avoided by behaving well. This matters beyond the statistics, because people who get cancer are routinely asked, and routinely ask themselves, what they did wrong.

Think of it like this

It is like road deaths. Seatbelts, speed limits and drink driving laws prevent an enormous number, and it would still be wrong to tell someone hit by a falling tree that they should have driven more carefully.

Preventable and blameworthy are not the same word, and the difference is not a technicality.

Why risk rises so steeply with age, all together now

This course can now answer its own opening question properly, and the answer has three parts that are usually collapsed into one.

The World Health Organization (WHO) gives two of them, and hedges them, which is worth copying exactly. Incidence rises dramatically with age, it says, most likely because of cumulative exposure to risk factors that increase with age, combined with the tendency for cellular repair mechanisms to become less effective as a person grows older. Both are about damage: more of it arriving, less of it being fixed. Neither is stated as settled.

Yoshida supplies the third and it is the one that is easy to miss. Time is not just when damage accumulates, it is also what clonal expansion needs. A driver mutation acquired at eight is not dangerous at eight. It is dangerous at fifty-eight, because for fifty years its descendants have been quietly increasing in number, and each of those cells is another chance for the next driver mutation to land somewhere that already has a head start. The clock is not only counting damage. It is counting opportunity.

Think of it like this

Compound interest, running on something you would rather was not compounding. The rate is small and the term is the entire problem.

Age raises risk three separate ways: more damage, worse repair, and more time for clones to grow.

Test yourself

Think about it: using these numbers responsibly

  1. Someone reads that 38 per cent of cancers are preventable and concludes that people who get cancer mostly caused it. Point out exactly where that reasoning breaks.

    In at least three places. First, 38 per cent preventable at population level says nothing about which individual cases were preventable, and there is no way to assign an individual case to the preventable fraction. Second, most of the named risk factors are strongly patterned by income, occupation and where someone lives, so a large share of what looks like choice is circumstance. Third, and most fundamentally, the mutational clock runs regardless: the clock-like signatures SBS1 (single base substitution signature 1) and SBS5 (single base substitution signature 5) accumulate with age in every tissue in every person. A meaningful part of cancer risk is not attached to any behaviour at all.

  2. Lung cancer causes 2.6 million new cases and 1.86 million deaths in the same year, while breast cancer causes 2.4 million cases and 694,000 deaths. What can you legitimately conclude, and what would you be overreaching to conclude?

    You can legitimately say that in the same year, lung cancer killed far more people than breast cancer despite a similar number of new cases, and that this points at some combination of worse survival and later diagnosis. You would be overreaching to convert this directly into survival rates, because cases and deaths in a single year are not the same cohort: the people dying this year were mostly diagnosed in earlier years. Comparing two snapshots is suggestive, not a measurement.

The general rule: a ratio without a denominator, and a snapshot without a cohort, are the two ways health statistics most often mislead.

This course, the fundamentals, covers what happens before a cancer exists: how mutations accumulate in healthy people, why almost none of them lead anywhere, and how long the whole process takes.

What this course is not, stated plainly

It is not medical advice and it cannot be used as any. Nothing here describes how to diagnose, screen for or treat a cancer in a person, and no figure in it applies to an individual.

It is not comprehensive. It is built on one review of one part of the subject, chosen because it answers the beginner's real question better than a textbook chapter would. Whole areas are missing: how the immune system deals with abnormal cells, how metastasis actually works step by step, how any treatment works. Some are covered in the next two courses and some are not covered at all.

And it is a synthesis, not a source. For anything you intend to rely on in your own work, go to the papers listed two slides back, and from those follow the citations to the primary research underneath. A review is a map. It is not the territory, and no course built on one is either.

One review, read properly, honestly labelled. That is what this is and all it is.
